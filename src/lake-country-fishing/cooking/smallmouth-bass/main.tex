\section{Filleting}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cut down behind the gill plate} and the pectoral fin, angling slightly toward the head, until the blade meets the backbone. Do not cut through it.
\item \textbf{Turn the blade flat} and run it along the top of the backbone to the tail in one long stroke, letting the spine guide the knife. Leave the fillet attached at the tail so it acts as a handle.
\item \textbf{Slice the rib cage out} in a shallow arc, taking as little flesh with it as you can.
\item \textbf{Skin it:} fillet skin-side down, grip the tail, run the knife between skin and flesh at a slight downward angle while pulling the skin against the blade.
\item Flip the fish and repeat. Rinse in cold water, pat dry, and get it onto ice.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bones and what to watch for}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Rib cage}{Removed at step 3. Nothing else to deal with.}
\telosfact{Red strip}{Less pronounced than on largemouth, but still worth trimming.}
\telosfact{Skin}{Comes away cleanly and is not worth eating.}
\telosfact{Check by feel}{Run a finger tip-to-tail before cooking.}
\end{telosfacts}

Pan-fried & Flour, salt, pepper, butter. Three minutes a side. Smallmouth is clean enough that heavy seasoning is a waste of it.\\
En papillote & Fillet on parchment with lemon, dill, a knob of butter and a splash of white wine. Fold and seal, \qty{400}{^\circ F}, twelve minutes. Opens at the table.\\
Over a fire & A whole gutted smallmouth, scored, stuffed with lemon and herbs, wrapped in foil and buried in coals for twenty minutes. The best thing you can do with one on the shore of the lake it came from.\\

\begin{telospanel}{Bottom matters}
A fish tastes of where it lives. Smallmouth from Pine Lake's clean gravel are consistently better than largemouth from the same lake's soft weedy bays --- same water, different bottom, different flavour. If you want to know how a lake will eat before you cook it, look at what is under the fish. \sheetref{Reading Water}
\end{telospanel}
