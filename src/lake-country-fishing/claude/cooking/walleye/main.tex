\begin{eatingfacts}
\telosfact{Verdict}{The reason people drive to Wisconsin. Firm, white, large-flaked, and so mild that the fish tastes of what you cook it in. \telosrating{5}}
\telosfact{Texture}{Dense and moist, holds together in a pan where a bluegill falls apart. Forgiving of slight overcooking.}
\telosfact{Oil}{Low. Keeps poorly frozen past about four months --- the flavour goes flat rather than fishy.}
\telosfact{Best size}{18--22\,in, which is also the legal window here. A very large walleye is coarser and worth releasing anyway.}
\telosfact{Yield}{About 35\% of whole weight as skinless fillet. A 20\,in fish gives two fillets around 6\,oz each --- one generous portion.}
\telosfact{Skin}{Always remove it. Walleye skin is tough and does not crisp.}
\end{eatingfacts}

\section{Filleting}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Cut down behind the gill plate}, angling slightly toward the head, until you feel the backbone. Do not cut through it.
\item \textbf{Turn the blade flat} and run it along the top of the backbone from head to tail, letting the spine guide the knife. One long stroke beats twenty short ones. Leave the fillet attached at the tail as a handle.
\item \textbf{Lift the fillet and slice the rib cage out} in a shallow arc, taking as little meat as you can. Or skip this and pull the rib bones with pliers after skinning.
\item \textbf{Skin it:} lay the fillet skin-side down, hold the tail, and run the knife between skin and flesh at a slight downward angle, sawing gently while pulling the skin.
\item \textbf{Take the cheeks.} Two coin-sized nuggets of the best meat on the fish, in the hollow behind and below the eye. Cut a semicircle through the skin, pop the meat out with the knife tip, peel the skin off. Most people throw these away. Do not be most people.
\item Rinse in cold water, pat dry, and get it on ice or in the pan.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bones}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Rib cage}{The only bones to deal with, and step 3 removes them.}
\telosfact{No Y-bones}{Unlike pike, a walleye fillet is genuinely boneless once the ribs are out. \sheetref{Cooking Northern Pike}}
\telosfact{Run a finger}{Along the fillet before cooking, tip to tail, feeling for anything sharp. Thirty seconds now saves a bad mouthful later.}
\end{telosfacts}

\section{Three ways to cook it}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.18\linewidth}L@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{How}\\
\midrule
Pan-fried & The standard, and the best. Dry the fillet hard. Dredge in seasoned flour, or beaten egg then cracker crumbs. Medium-high heat, butter and a little oil so the butter does not burn. Three minutes a side for a half-inch fillet. Lemon. Nothing else.\\
Beer-battered & For a fish fry. Flour, cornstarch, baking powder, cold beer, whisked to thin-cream consistency. \qty{375}{^\circ F} oil, three to four minutes to golden. Drain on a rack, never on paper --- paper steams the crust off.\\
Baked whole fillets & For a bigger batch. \qty{400}{^\circ F}, butter, lemon slices, paprika, ten to twelve minutes. Done when it flakes at a gentle push and the flesh has just turned opaque.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\begin{telospanel}{The one rule}
Walleye is a mild fish and every minute past done makes it worse. Pull it when the centre has \emph{just} gone from glassy to opaque --- it carries on cooking off the heat. Overcooked walleye is dry and ordinary, which is a waste of the best fish in the lake.
\end{telospanel}
