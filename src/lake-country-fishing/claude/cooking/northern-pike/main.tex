\section{The five-fillet method --- how to beat the Y-bones}
A pike has an extra row of \textbf{Y-shaped intramuscular bones}, sitting above the lateral line in the \emph{front half} of each fillet only. They are floating bones --- they connect to nothing --- so you cannot pull them like pin bones. You cut around them instead, and you deliberately give up a strip of meat. That is the whole trick, and it takes about two minutes once you have done it three times.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Take two fillets normally.} Cut behind the gill plate to the backbone, turn the blade, run to the tail. Remove the ribs.
\item \textbf{Lay a fillet skin-side down.} Feel for the row of bone tips with a fingertip, running along the fillet slightly above the lateral line, from the head end back to roughly where the dorsal fin was --- about the front 60\% of the fillet. Behind that point there are none.
\item \textbf{Cut along the top of the Y-bones.} Starting at the head end, slice horizontally just \emph{above} the bone tips, keeping the blade flat against them, until the cut runs out where the bones stop. This frees the whole upper-front section: \textbf{fillet one}, boneless.
\item \textbf{Cut along the bottom of the Y-bones.} Same again, this time just \emph{below} the row, blade riding along the underside of the bones. This frees the lower-front section: \textbf{fillet two}, boneless.
\item \textbf{What is left} is a narrow strip containing every Y-bone, plus the rear third of the fillet which never had any. Cut the clean rear section off and keep it --- \textbf{fillet three}.
\item Repeat on the other side. Five boneless pieces from one fish, and one bony strip that goes in the stockpot or the bin.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Bones and what to watch for}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Y-bones}{Above the lateral line, front 60\% of the fillet only, floating and un-pullable. Cut above and below them, as in steps 3 and 4.}
\telosfact{Do not try pliers}{They break rather than pull, and you end up with bone fragments in the meat. This is the mistake everyone makes once.}
\telosfact{The alternative}{On smaller pike, score the fillet crossways every 3\,mm down to the skin and deep-fry hot. The bones are fine enough to dissolve and become unnoticeable. It works, but the five-fillet method is better.}
\telosfact{Pickling}{Traditional and effective --- the brine softens the Y-bones completely. Use a recipe with a boiling-water step, because the broad fish tapeworm is the one parasite in Wisconsin fish that matters to people.}
\end{telosfacts}

\section{Cooking it}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{0.18\linewidth}L@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{How}\\
\midrule
Beer-battered & Pike is firm enough to take a proper batter. \qty{375}{^\circ F}, three to four minutes. Many people who say they dislike pike have only ever eaten it badly cleaned.\\
Poached & The firm flake suits gentle cooking. Court-bouillon or milk with bay and peppercorns, eight minutes at a bare simmer. Cold with mayonnaise the next day is better still.\\
Pickled & Cube the flesh, brine in salt for 48 hours, rinse, then cover in a boiled and cooled vinegar-sugar-pickling-spice solution with sliced onion. Two weeks in the fridge. The classic Wisconsin treatment and it makes the Y-bone problem vanish entirely.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\begin{telospanel}{Do this once with somebody watching}
The five-fillet method is very hard to learn from words and very easy to learn from one demonstration. Do the first pike slowly, with a finger on the bone row the whole time so you can feel where the blade is. After two fish it becomes automatic, and pike goes from a fish people throw back to one of the best on the lake. \sheetref{Northern Pike}
\end{telospanel}
