\telosmasthead[Observe three field states]
\lessonhead{2}{two sessions of 45 minutes}{learner maps; adult manages filings}

\begin{materials}
\item Two labelled bar magnets
\item One small compass; a second compass for the proof test
\item Graph paper, ruler, pencil, and protractor
\item Clear acrylic or stiff card
\item Iron filings sealed in a shaker
\item Tape to mark fixed magnet positions
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
Draw the compass direction at the centre of each arrangement before testing:
one magnet; opposite poles facing; like poles facing. For each prediction,
write one observation that would make you change your mind.
\end{predict}

\magnetstates

\begin{telospanel}{Read the states before drawing lines}
\textbf{A.} Where is the compass turn largest? \quad
\textbf{B.} Does the centre needle point across or along the gap? \quad
\textbf{C.} At the balance point, does ``small response'' mean ``no field
anywhere''? Answer from nearby compass readings, not from filings alone.
\end{telospanel}

\lessonsection{First pass: reveal, then reset}
Tape magnet outlines to the bench so each state can be reproduced. The adult
shakes a sparse layer of filings onto the card; the learner taps the card,
sketches the pattern, and labels the magnet positions. Return filings to their
container before changing state. Keep filings away from eyes, electronics, and
the compass pivot. Filings show alignment and crowding, but they do not supply
arrow direction; the compass does.

\begin{telosframe}
\textbf{Evidence rule:} use filings for shape and crowding; use the compass for
arrow direction. A neat pattern cannot replace a bearing. Record all three
states in Worksheet A at the end.
\end{telosframe}

\newpage
\telosmasthead[Walk one compass; keep every trial]
\small
\lessonsection{Build a point-by-point map}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Tape one bar magnet on a graph-paper grid. Trace its outline and mark
which end is north. Do not move it until the table is complete.
\item Place the compass centre on a grid intersection, wait for the needle to
settle, and trace the north-pointing direction as an arrow.
\item Lift the compass completely, replace it at the same point, and repeat.
Record both bearings. A repeat is evidence about reliability, not busywork.
\item Continue around the magnet. Use the same compass, reading position,
settling time, and grid spacing at every point.
\item Only after all arrows exist, add faint curves tangent to them. Never bend
a curve merely to make the picture look symmetrical.
\end{enumerate}

\compasswalk

\begin{teloscard}{Questions while the map is still raw}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Which two points should match by symmetry? Do their repeated bearings
agree within your reading precision?
\item Near a pole, does the needle settle faster, slower, or no differently?
Record the observation; do not turn settling speed directly into field strength.
\item If two nearby arrows disagree sharply, should a smooth curve pass through
both? What repeat would decide which arrow to trust?
\end{enumerate}
\end{teloscard}

\lessonsection{Repeated bearing record}
Measure bearings clockwise from the top edge of the paper. Use identical grid
points for all repeats. Enter the six point records in Worksheet B at the end;
do not erase discordant readings.

\begin{telospanel}{Stop and judge the repeats}
Circle the largest bearing difference. If it exceeds \(10^\circ\), make a third
reading before drawing any guide curve. Check first for a shifted magnet,
nearby steel, a tilted compass, or a grid point read from the wrong side.
\end{telospanel}

\telosmasthead[Turn observations into a model]
\small
\mapstates

\lessonsection{Worked inference}
At the grid point \((x,y)=(0,\qty{3}{cm})\), suppose two north-needle bearings
are \(88^\circ\) and \(92^\circ\). Their mean is
\[
  (88^\circ+92^\circ)/2=90^\circ ,
\]
and their spread is \(4^\circ\). The defensible plotted result is an arrow at
about \(90^\circ\), with the repeat spread noted. It supports a horizontal
field direction at that point. It does \emph{not} measure field magnitude:
a freely rotating compass reports direction, while its turn angle relative to
the paper depends on the combined magnet and Earth's fields.

\begin{teloscard}{A second inference: comparison, not a law}
Suppose a compass initially points \(0^\circ\) with the magnet absent. On the
same axis it turns to \(58^\circ\) at \(\qty{2}{cm}\), \(31^\circ\) at
\(\qty{4}{cm}\), and \(17^\circ\) at \(\qty{6}{cm}\). The response decreases
with distance. That trend is supported. ``The field halves whenever distance
doubles'' is not: \(58^\circ\) to \(31^\circ\) is only one comparison, and
compass angle is not directly proportional to magnetic-field strength.
\end{teloscard}

\lessonsection{Change one state, preserve the test}
Repeat four fixed grid points for opposite poles facing and like poles facing.
Use the same gap and compass.

Enter the four matched-point comparisons in Worksheet C at the end.

\begin{telospanel}{Intermediate claim check}
For each state, write: ``At point \_\_, the needle pointed \_\_; therefore the
field direction there was \_\_.'' Then add: ``This does not yet tell me
\_\_.'' A strong claim says both what the observation establishes and what it
leaves open.
\end{telospanel}

\lessonsection{Electric-field analogy}
The map-making rule transfers: an electric-field arrow gives the force
direction on a positive test charge at one location. The probes differ.
A compass samples a magnetic field; it does not sample electric field.
Electric-field lines begin on positive charge and end on negative charge,
whereas magnetic-field lines form closed loops. In both cases, drawn lines are
a model assembled from local evidence, not visible threads in space.

\lessonsection{Final proof: predict a hidden reading}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose a blank grid point between four measured points. Without placing
a compass there, draw the direction your map predicts. Write a tolerance,
such as ``within \(10^\circ\).''
\item Give the sheet and a second compass to another person. Hide your
prediction with a card while they centre the compass and record two bearings.
\item Reveal the prediction. Compute the smallest angular difference between
prediction and each reading. A prediction of \(350^\circ\) and reading of
\(5^\circ\) differ by \(15^\circ\), not \(345^\circ\).
\item Repeat at a point where the map bends sharply. State whether each result
falls within tolerance. If not, revise the local curve or collect more points;
do not revise the recorded bearings.
\end{enumerate}

Write the prediction, tolerance, hidden readings, differences, and decision in
the Proof record at the end. Preserve the prediction exactly as written before
the compass is revealed.

\lessonsection{What the proof establishes}
A successful hidden prediction shows that the field map compresses earlier
measurements well enough to predict a new local direction. It does not prove
that the drawn curves are physical strands, that the map is exact between all
points, or that magnetic and electric fields are the same thing.

\begin{telospanel}{The link to the coil}
The primary coil creates a magnetic field; the secondary responds to the field
at its own location. Later lessons change that field with time. This map has
already established the essential discipline: fix the source, sample defined
locations, preserve repeats, infer only what the probe can show, then demand a
new prediction.
\end{telospanel}
\begin{telosframe}
\textbf{This lesson uses no electrical power source.} Keep loose filings away
from eyes, electronics, and the compass pivot. The habits that matter here are
experimental: fix the source, change one state at a time, preserve every
reading, and test the finished model against a new observation.
\end{telosframe}
