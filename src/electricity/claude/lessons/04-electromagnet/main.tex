\telosmasthead[direction is not strength]
\lessonhead{4}{two 55-minute sessions}{staged build; battery power only}

\begin{materials}
\item Enamelled magnet wire, 26--30 AWG, about 4\,m
\item Iron bolt and a loose-fitting cardboard sleeve
\item Two AA cells in a fused, switched holder
\item 12 identical paper clips in one cup
\item Compass and multimeter
\item Sandpaper, tape, ruler, timer, index card
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
A \(30\)-turn coil lifts some paper clips. Predict what will happen when you
(a) add \(30\) turns, (b) remove the iron core, and (c) reverse the current.
For each, predict both \emph{strength} and \emph{pole direction}. Do not use
``stronger'' without naming the observation that would count as stronger.
\end{predict}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Roll and tape the cardboard sleeve so it slides over the bolt without
  wobbling. Mark a \(45\,\mathrm{mm}\) winding zone.
\item Wind \(30\) adjacent turns in one direction. Tape the first and last
  turns. Do not wind directly on the bolt: the removable sleeve makes a fair
  iron-versus-air comparison possible.
\item With the battery disconnected, the adult checks that only the two lead
  tips are bare. Measure \(R_{30}\) and enter it in the end worksheet. An open
  display means enamel or a broken wire; near zero means a bypass around the
  winding.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{telospanel}{Checkpoint}
Why must the winding zone, wire gauge, bolt, and test pole stay the same when
turn count changes? Which of those could change resistance as well as geometry?
\end{telospanel}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Put the bolt into the sleeve. Use the index card to reset the lower pole
  \(5\,\mathrm{mm}\) above the same cup of clips before every run.
\item Close the switch for \(3.0\,\mathrm{s}\), raise the coil vertically, and
  count clips still attached. Open the switch immediately. Wait \(30\,\mathrm{s}\)
  and return all clips before the next run.
\item Complete four baseline trials. If the spread is more than two clips,
  improve the method before comparing designs. Record every run in the
  end-of-lesson worksheet.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Pause and decide.} What caused the largest uncontrolled
difference between trials? Would a one-clip improvement be larger than your
measurement noise?

Run four trials for each row. Between rows, name the one intended change aloud.
For test D, hold a compass one bolt-length from the upper pole and record its
direction before and after reversing the leads. Use rows A--D in the
end-of-lesson worksheet.

\begin{telospanel}{Three questions before interpreting}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Did \(60\) turns also change current? Use measured \(NI\), not turns
  alone, when comparing the two windings.
\item Does the iron-core difference exceed the ranges of both repeated sets?
\item Did reversing current change compass direction, lifting strength, both,
  or neither?
\end{enumerate}
\end{telospanel}

\lessonsection{What the measurements mean}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Current makes field}{Moving charge produces a magnetic field. Around
one straight conductor the field circles the wire; in a coil, the fields of
successive turns reinforce along the axis.}
\telosfact{Ampere-turns matter}{The useful comparison is \(NI\): turns
multiplied by measured current. Added wire also adds resistance, so doubling
turns does not guarantee double current-times-turns or double lifting force.}
\telosfact{Iron changes the path}{Iron magnetizes strongly and concentrates
flux through the bolt and the small air gap. The core test isolates that effect
only because sleeve, coil, gap, clips, and pulse remain fixed.}
\telosfact{Direction is not strength}{Reversing current swaps the poles. With
the same current magnitude and geometry, it need not produce a large strength
change. The compass and the clip count answer different questions.}
\end{telosfacts}

\begin{telospanel}{Evidence gate}
Before calculating a mean, mark each run \(\circ\) accept or \(\times\) repeat.
Repeat only for a stated procedural failure---wrong gap, wrong pulse time,
dropped clip, or disturbed coil---never because the number looks inconvenient.
\end{telospanel}

\lessonsection{Final evidence proof}
Write one conclusion that your measurements, rather than the expected theory,
force you to accept.
\[
\boxed{\text{single planned change}}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{difference in repeated data}}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{difference exceeds spread}}
\longrightarrow
\boxed{\text{limited conclusion}}
\]

\noindent Planned change: \rule{42mm}{.4pt}\quad
Measured difference: \rule{48mm}{.4pt}

\noindent Ranges compared: \rule{38mm}{.4pt}\quad
Conclusion justified: \rule{65mm}{.4pt}

\begin{telospanel}{Disproof test}
Name a possible next result that would make you withdraw or narrow that
conclusion. A prediction confirmed once is not proof; a claim that survives a
fair opportunity to fail is stronger evidence.
\end{telospanel}
