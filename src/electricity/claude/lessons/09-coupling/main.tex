\telosmasthead[The lesson that explains why a Tesla coil is not a transformer]
\lessonhead{9}{about 75 minutes}{everyone builds; battery power only}

\begin{materials}
\item Two identical pendulums --- same length, same weight
\item A horizontal string or dowel to hang them from
\item Two identical LC tanks from Lesson 8
\item Signal generator and oscilloscope, or the multimeter method
\item A ruler, to set and measure coil spacing
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
Two identical pendulums hang from the same slack string. You start one swinging
and hold the other still, then let go. What happens to each over the next
minute? Draw both, one under the other, against time.
\end{predict}

\lessonsection{Do it --- the pendulums first}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Hang two identical pendulums from a common horizontal string with some
  slack in it. Start one; leave the other at rest.
\item Watch for a full minute. The first slows and stops; the second, which
  nobody touched, is now swinging at full amplitude. Then it reverses, and the
  energy comes back. It keeps trading, indefinitely, until friction wins.
\item \textbf{Change the coupling.} Tighten the string. The trade happens faster.
  Slacken it right off and the second pendulum barely moves at all.
\item \textbf{Break the match.} Shorten one pendulum so the two no longer share
  a frequency. Now almost nothing transfers, at any coupling. \emph{Matching
  matters more than coupling.}
\end{enumerate}

\lessonsection{Then the circuits}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Set up two identical LC tanks, tuned to the same $f_0$, with their coils a
  few centimetres apart and coaxial.
\item Drive the first at $f_0$ and measure the voltage across the second.
\item Slide them closer. The transfer rises and the single sharp peak splits
  into \emph{two} peaks either side of $f_0$ --- an over-coupled pair no longer
  has one resonance, it has two.
\item Detune one tank with a different capacitor. Transfer collapses, exactly as
  with the shortened pendulum.
\end{enumerate}

\lessonsection{What it means}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Coupling coefficient}{Written $k$, from 0 (no shared field) to 1 (all
  of it shared). An iron-cored mains transformer runs at $k \approx 0.95$. A
  Tesla coil runs deliberately \emph{loose}, around $k = 0.1$ to $0.2$.}
\telosfact{Why loose is better here}{Energy takes many cycles to cross a loosely
  coupled pair, and during those cycles the secondary's voltage builds and
  builds. Couple them tightly and the transfer is fast but the secondary never
  gets the chance to ring up. Tight coupling also puts so much voltage across
  the secondary's own turns that it arcs internally and destroys itself.}
\telosfact{Both must be tuned the same}{This is what the pendulum experiment
  proves. Coupling with mismatched frequencies transfers almost nothing.}
\telosfact{Energy goes both ways}{The pendulums traded back and forth, and so do
  the coils. In a real coil we want the transfer stopped at the moment the
  secondary is fullest --- which is exactly what the spark gap does in
  Lesson 10, by quenching.}
\telosfact{This is why it is not a transformer}{A transformer trades voltage for
  current by turns ratio alone. A Tesla coil does that \emph{and} accumulates
  energy over many cycles of a shared resonance. That second mechanism is where
  most of the voltage comes from.}
\end{telosfacts}

\begin{telospanel}{The whole machine, in one sentence you can now parse}
The transformer charges the capacitor; the gap dumps it into the primary tank,
which rings at $f_0$; the secondary is tuned to the same $f_0$ and loosely
coupled, so over many cycles it takes that energy and rings up to a voltage far
higher than the transformer alone could make; and the gap quenches at the right
moment so the energy does not slosh back. Lessons 6, 7, 8 and 9 are those four
clauses.
\end{telospanel}
