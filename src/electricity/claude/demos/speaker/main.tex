\begin{materials}
\item Magnet wire, 28--32 AWG, about 10\,m
\item A paper or plastic cup, or a stiff card disc
\item Two or three neodymium disc magnets
\item A 3.5\,mm audio cable you are willing to cut, or a headphone socket breakout
\item A phone or small amplifier as the source
\item Tape, sandpaper, a round former (a AAA battery or a marker pen)
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
A speaker has no moving parts you can see and no valves. Music is a pattern of
air pressure. What has to happen, physically, to turn a wire carrying a changing
current into a pattern of air pressure? Write it in one sentence before you
build.
\end{predict}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.22]
\telosfact{Coil in a field}{Exactly Demo A and Lesson 4: current in a magnetic field feels a force. Here the current is the audio signal, changing thousands of times a second.}
\telosfact{Push and pull}{Because audio current alternates, the force alternates. The coil is pushed away and pulled back, hundreds or thousands of times a second.}
\telosfact{Moving air}{The coil is glued to a cone. The cone moves air. Moving air is sound. That is the entire chain, and there is nothing else in a loudspeaker.}
\telosfact{It works backwards too}{Speak into it and the cone moves the coil in the field, inducing a small voltage --- Lesson 5. A speaker is a microphone run in reverse, and you can prove it with the multimeter.}
\end{telosfacts}

\lessonsection{Build it}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Wind the coil} around a AAA battery or a marker pen: 40--60 turns, all the same direction, leaving 20\,cm tails. Slide it off and tape it so it holds its ring shape.
\item \textbf{Sand the enamel} off both tails until bright copper shows. This is the step that gets skipped and it is why it will not work.
\item \textbf{Tape the coil flat} to the bottom of the cup, centred.
\item \textbf{Put the magnets under it}, close but not touching --- 1 to 2\,mm. The coil must be free to move.
\item \textbf{Connect the tails} to the audio cable's signal and ground conductors, and play something loud with a lot of bass.
\item Hold the cup lightly. You will feel it before you hear it.
\end{enumerate}

\lessonsection{Break it}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.22]
\telosfact{More turns}{Louder --- more current-carrying wire in the field. Also more resistance. Find where it stops helping.}
\telosfact{Move the magnet}{Further away is quieter, and the fall-off is steep. Same distance-dependence as the eddy-current pendulum. \sheetref{Demo: Eddy Currents}}
\telosfact{Change the cone}{A big stiff card is louder and better at low notes; a small cup is quieter and thinner. This is the whole of loudspeaker design in one experiment.}
\telosfact{Run it backwards}{Disconnect the audio, connect the multimeter on millivolts AC, and shout into the cup. You are looking at your own voice as a voltage.}
\telosfact{Volume}{Keep it modest. An unloaded coil is close to a short circuit and a phone output will not enjoy it.}
\end{telosfacts}

\begin{telospanel}{The point}
Every loudspeaker in the house --- phone, television, car, headphones --- is this
object, made precisely. Once a teenager has built one out of a cup, the sentence
``a speaker is a coil in a magnetic field glued to a piece of paper'' stops
being a claim and becomes something they have held in their hand.
\end{telospanel}
