\section{Read the event without making one}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.92\linewidth,height=2.45in,keepaspectratio]{%
  electricity/chatgpt/lessons/10-spark-gap/assets/gas-breakdown-pencil.png}
\par\footnotesize\emph{Conceptual graphite cutaway. It identifies stages, not
dimensions, parts, or a construction method.}
\end{center}

Before breakdown, a few mobile charges already exist through background
ionization. An electric field accelerates them. If a collision transfers enough
energy to free another electron, the number of charge carriers can grow. Losses
to walls, attachment, and recombination compete with that growth. A self-
sustaining avalanche creates a conducting channel; stored energy can then move
through it rapidly. The channel heats, glows, expands, and eventually recovers
as charge density and temperature fall.

\begin{telosfacts}[.25]
\telosfact{Necessary}{An electric field, seed charge carriers, and collision
conditions that permit net multiplication.}
\telosfact{Not sufficient}{A visible gap by itself. Gas, pressure, electrode
shape, contamination, polarity, and history also matter.}
\telosfact{What light proves}{Excited atoms and molecules released photons. It
does not by itself reveal voltage, current, or delivered energy.}
\telosfact{What sound proves}{The gas changed pressure rapidly. Loudness is not
a safe proxy for electrical energy.}
\end{telosfacts}

\section{Read a breakdown curve}

For a fixed gas and electrode system, a Paschen-style graph relates breakdown
voltage to the product of pressure \(p\) and separation \(d\). The axes below
are deliberately dimensionless and the values are invented for graph-reading
practice. They are not design data.

\subsection{Worked reading 1: same distance, different pressure}
Two sealed historical tubes have the same separation. Tube A is plotted at
\(pd=0.5\); tube B at \(pd=5\). Reading vertically from the practice curve gives
about 130 invented voltage units for A and about 320 for B. The separation did
not change, yet the breakdown condition did. Therefore ``gap alone sets the
voltage'' is falsified by this pair.

\subsection{Worked reading 2: two products, one approximate voltage}
A horizontal line at 200 invented units crosses the curve on both sides of the
minimum. One reading has relatively few effective collisions; the other has
frequent collisions but shorter energy-gaining flights. A single observed
breakdown voltage therefore need not identify a unique \(pd\) product.

\Needspace{14\baselineskip}
\begin{teloscard}{Graph-reading questions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=.35em]
\item At the marked minimum, would moving either left or right lower the
breakdown value? \emph{No. Both directions raise it on this model.}
\item If pressure doubles and distance halves, what happens to \(pd\)?
\emph{It stays constant; this curve predicts the same point, within its scope.}
\item Does the curve predict all electrode shapes and gas mixtures?
\emph{No. Those conditions define which curve is relevant.}
\item A datum lies far from the curve. Is ``bad measurement'' the only
explanation? \emph{No: wrong gas, pressure error, surface condition, geometry,
or an inadequate model are competing explanations.}
\end{enumerate}
\end{teloscard}

The gap supplies no energy. It changes state and permits previously stored field
energy to move into a rapidly changing current. Some energy enters the intended
electromagnetic oscillation; some becomes electrode heating, hot gas, light,
sound, chemical products, and unintended radio emission. Historical utility
does not imply present safety or spectrum compatibility.

\begin{teloscard}{Worked energy account}
Suppose a museum placard says that 10 abstract energy units were stored before
an event. A calorimetric reconstruction attributes 4 units to heating, 1 to
light and sound, and 3 to the intended oscillation. The missing 2 units are not
``destroyed.'' They set a measurement question: unmeasured radio emission,
apparatus heating, residual stored energy, or uncertainty. A defensible claim
is \emph{accounted energy is 8 units}; it is not \emph{efficiency is exactly
30\%} until the system boundary and uncertainties are stated.
\end{teloscard}

\section{Claims that evidence can defeat}

\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}p{.27\linewidth}p{.34\linewidth}L@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Claim} & \textbf{Discriminating observation} & \textbf{Conclusion}\\
\midrule
Gap alone determines breakdown & Same separation and gas, controlled pressure
changed; breakdown value changed & Gap-only model is false.\\
The spark creates energy & Stored energy is reduced while heat, light, sound,
and field energy appear & The gap redirects stored energy.\\
Brighter means more voltage & Brightness changes with current duration or
camera exposure at comparable voltage & Brightness is not a voltmeter.\\
One curve fits every gas & A second gas produces a reproducibly shifted curve
under matched geometry & Gas identity belongs in the model.\\
Every failed event proves insulation & Independent field and pressure checks
show conditions were below the model's threshold & Only that tested condition
is supported.\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}

\begin{gate}{A safe falsification boundary}
Learners falsify claims by comparing supplied records, reading graphs, checking
units, and identifying alternative explanations. They do not ``test the
threshold,'' shorten a gap, increase a supply, charge a store, or defeat a
museum enclosure. A result that requires exposure to electrical danger is not
a learner result.
\end{gate}

\section{Verify a reported result}

Use this sequence on a paper dataset:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Scope.} Record gas, pressure measure, separation measure,
electrode description, polarity, temperature, and stated uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Axes.} Check whether the horizontal coordinate is \(p\), \(d\),
or their product, and whether either axis is logarithmic.
\item \textbf{Replicates.} Look for spread, conditioning, and order effects;
one event is not a curve.
\item \textbf{Competing accounts.} Ask what contamination, surface changes,
pressure drift, or instrument limits would predict.
\item \textbf{Residual.} Plot observed minus modeled values. Random scatter and
a systematic bend tell different stories.
\item \textbf{Claim.} State only what the measured range supports.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{teloscard}{Worked verification question}
A report gives ten breakdown values but omits pressure and electrode geometry.
Can the Paschen-style model be verified? \emph{No.} The values may be real, but
the report lacks variables needed to locate the observations on the model and
to decide whether the same curve applies. The correct next step is to request
metadata, not to reproduce the event.
\end{teloscard}

\section{Change one thing --- on paper}
Choose one supplied curve point. Predict how its plotted position changes if
pressure doubles while separation is halved. Then write:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item the quantity held constant;
\item one assumption the graph requires;
\item one observation that would falsify your prediction; and
\item one reason the observation must come from a qualified laboratory or
published dataset rather than a learner-built apparatus.
\end{enumerate}
