\telosmasthead[Make the invisible visible]
\lessonhead{2}{about 60 minutes}{everyone builds; no electrical hazard}

\begin{materials}
\item Two bar magnets, one horseshoe magnet
\item Iron filings in a shaker (or steel wool cut fine)
\item A sheet of clear acrylic or stiff card
\item Several small compasses
\item Castor or mineral oil in a shallow clear dish
\item Grass seed or fine semolina
\item The electroscope from Lesson 1, and a charged comb
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
You will lay a card over a bar magnet and sprinkle iron filings on it. Draw,
before you do it, what you think the pattern will look like. Keep the drawing.
Then draw what you think happens with \emph{two} magnets, north facing north.
\end{predict}

\lessonsection{Do it}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{One magnet.} Lay the card over a bar magnet. Sprinkle filings
  thinly and tap the card gently. Compare with your drawing.
\item \textbf{Two magnets, attracting.} North facing south, a few centimetres
  apart. Sprinkle and tap. The lines run straight from one to the other.
\item \textbf{Two magnets, repelling.} North facing north. The lines curve away
  and a region between them stays almost empty.
\item \textbf{Walk a compass.} Put a single compass on the card and move it
  slowly around the magnet, drawing an arrow at each position. Twenty arrows
  later you have drawn the field yourself, one point at a time --- and that is
  what a field \emph{is}: a direction and a strength at every point in space.
\item \textbf{The electric field.} Float grass seed on a thin layer of oil in
  the dish. Bring a well-charged comb near the surface. The seeds line up along
  the electric field. It is fainter and fussier than the magnetic version ---
  which is exactly why magnetism was understood first.
\end{enumerate}

\lessonsection{What it means}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{The problem a field solves}{The magnet moves the compass without
  touching it. Either the force jumps across the gap instantly --- which turns
  out to be false --- or the magnet changes the \emph{space around it}, and the
  compass responds to the space where it sits. The second is true, and the
  changed space is the field.}
\telosfact{Reading a field map}{Direction of the line is the direction of the
  force on a north pole. Where lines crowd together the field is strong; where
  they spread out it is weak.}
\telosfact{Lines never cross}{If they did, a compass at the crossing would have
  to point two ways at once.}
\telosfact{Closed loops}{Magnetic field lines have no ends --- follow one and it
  returns through the magnet. Electric field lines do have ends: they start on
  positive charge and finish on negative.}
\telosfact{Sharp points concentrate}{Field lines crowd at corners and points.
  This is why a lightning rod is pointed and why a Tesla coil's topload is a
  smooth torus --- a smooth shape holds more charge before the air breaks down.}
\end{telosfacts}

\lessonsection{Break it}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Distance}{Move the compass twice as far away. Does the deflection
  halve, quarter, or something else? You cannot settle this by eye --- notice
  that, and notice that it will need a measurement.}
\telosfact{Shielding}{Put a sheet of steel between the magnet and the compass,
  then aluminium, then cardboard. Only one of them changes anything. Why?}
\telosfact{Cut it}{Break a cheap magnet in half. Do you get a separate north and
  a separate south, or two smaller complete magnets? Predict first.}
\end{telosfacts}

\begin{telospanel}{The link to the coil}
Every part of the coil is a field doing work. The primary makes a magnetic
field; the secondary sits in it and responds to it. The capacitor stores energy
in an electric field between two plates. The streamer happens when the electric
field near the topload gets strong enough to rip air apart. Nothing in the
machine touches anything else --- it is all fields.
\end{telospanel}
