\telosmeta{Electricity \& Magnetism}{5. Induction}{A \emph{changing} magnetic field makes a voltage}{2026-07-25}
\begin{document}
\telosmasthead[The one idea the whole machine rests on]
\lessonhead{5}{about 90 minutes}{everyone builds; no external power at all}

\begin{materials}
\item Magnet wire, 26--30 AWG, 30\,m or more
\item A cardboard tube (kitchen roll) and a length of copper pipe
\item Strong neodymium magnets that drop freely through both
\item Multimeter with a millivolt range
\item Two LEDs wired back-to-back
\item An aluminium tube, if you have one
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
You drop a magnet down a copper pipe. Copper is not magnetic and the magnet
never touches the walls. Does it fall faster than, slower than, or the same as a
magnet dropped down a cardboard tube of the same size? Commit before you drop
anything.
\end{predict}

\lessonsection{Do it}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Wind a sense coil.} Wind 200--400 turns of magnet wire around the
  cardboard tube. Sand the ends and connect them to the multimeter on its
  millivolt DC range.
\item \textbf{Drop the magnet.} Drop it through. The meter jumps and returns to
  zero.
\item \textbf{Hold it still.} Rest the magnet inside the coil and leave it
  there. The reading is \textbf{zero}. A magnetic field alone produces nothing.
\item \textbf{Change one thing at a time.} Drop it faster; use two magnets stuck
  together; turn the magnet over. Record the size and the \emph{sign} each time.
\item \textbf{Light an LED.} Replace the meter with two LEDs wired in opposite
  directions across the coil ends. Move the magnet in and out briskly. One LED
  flashes going in, the other coming out.
\item \textbf{The copper pipe.} Now drop a magnet down the cardboard tube and
  down the copper pipe, and time them. Compare with your prediction. Try the
  aluminium tube too.
\end{enumerate}

\lessonsection{What it means}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Faraday's law}{A voltage appears in a loop when the magnetic field
  \emph{through} that loop changes. Not when it is strong --- when it
  \emph{changes}. Faster change, bigger voltage; more turns, bigger voltage.}
\telosfact{Why holding it still gives nothing}{This is the whole lesson. A
  stationary magnet inside a coil produces exactly zero. Every generator, every
  transformer and every Tesla coil exists to make something change.}
\telosfact{Lenz's law}{The two LEDs flash on opposite motions because the
  induced current always opposes the change that made it. Push a magnet in and
  the coil pushes back.}
\telosfact{The copper pipe}{The falling magnet induces circulating eddy currents
  in the pipe wall. Those currents make their own magnetic field, which by
  Lenz's law opposes the fall. The magnet falls in slow motion. Nothing touches
  it and copper is not magnetic --- it is being braked by a field that its own
  falling created. This is the most surprising thing in the course, and it is
  worth doing several times.}
\telosfact{Conservation}{The energy in the flash came from your arm. Moving a
  magnet into a coil is \emph{harder} when the circuit is closed than when it is
  open. Try it with the LEDs connected and disconnected and feel the difference.}
\end{telosfacts}

\begin{telospanel}{The link to the coil --- read this one twice}
A Tesla coil's secondary is a coil sitting inside the primary's magnetic field.
When the spark gap fires, the primary's current swings from maximum to zero and
back hundreds of thousands of times a second. That violently changing field
induces a voltage in the secondary, and because the secondary has roughly a
thousand times more turns, and because both circuits are tuned to the same
frequency so the effect builds up over many cycles, the result is hundreds of
thousands of volts. Lessons 6, 8 and 9 each add one clause to that sentence.
Every one of them depends on what you just measured: \emph{change}, not
strength.
\end{telospanel}
