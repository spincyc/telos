\telosmasthead[Read the Safety sheet before this lesson, not during it]
\lessonhead{10}{about 60 minutes}{\textbf{adult-operated from here on}}

\highvoltagenote

\begin{materials}
\item A piezo igniter from a gas lighter (safe, and the only source the boys handle)
\item Two smooth machine-screw electrodes with adjustable spacing
\item A dry wooden or PVC stand
\item Feeler gauges or a ruler
\item Safety glasses for everyone
\item \emph{Only for the adult:} the neon-sign transformer, in its enclosure
\end{materials}

\begin{predict}
Air is an excellent insulator. What has to be true for a spark to cross a gap at
all --- and if you double the gap, does it take twice the voltage, more than
twice, or less?
\end{predict}

\lessonsection{Do it --- the safe version first}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Mount two rounded electrodes facing each other on a dry insulating stand,
  set to about 1\,mm.
\item Connect a piezo igniter across them. Click it. A spark jumps, with a snap.
  A piezo makes thousands of volts at almost no current, so this is safe to
  handle and it demonstrates the whole principle.
\item Widen the gap in steps and keep clicking. At some spacing the piezo can no
  longer bridge it. Record that distance --- you have just measured the
  breakdown voltage of air in units of piezo clicks.
\item \textbf{Sharp versus round.} Swap one rounded electrode for a needle. It
  now fires at a wider gap, and you may see a faint corona glow before any spark
  at all. Field lines crowd at a point, exactly as in Lesson 2.
\item \textbf{Listen and smell.} The snap is air heated so fast it explodes ---
  that is sound made by a spark. The sharp smell afterwards is ozone. Ventilate.
\end{enumerate}

\lessonsection{What it means}
\begin{telosfacts}[0.20]
\telosfact{Breakdown}{Air is an insulator until the electric field rips
  electrons off its molecules. Those electrons accelerate, hit more molecules,
  free more electrons --- an avalanche. In under a microsecond a column of air
  becomes a conductor.}
\telosfact{The number}{Dry air breaks down at roughly \qty{30}{kV} per
  centimetre at sea level, so about \qty{3}{kV} per millimetre. That is a rule
  of thumb, not a constant: humidity, temperature, altitude, electrode shape and
  surface roughness all move it.}
\telosfact{Not quite linear}{Doubling the gap needs a bit more than double the
  voltage, and below about a tenth of a millimetre the relationship reverses
  entirely --- Paschen's law. For our purposes: wider gap, higher firing
  voltage, and the gap spacing is therefore how you \emph{set} the voltage the
  capacitor charges to before it fires.}
\telosfact{Why it is a good switch}{It closes in well under a microsecond and
  carries enormous current with almost no resistance. No mechanical or
  semiconductor switch you can build at home comes close. That speed is what
  makes the primary tank ring hard.}
\telosfact{Quenching}{Once the gap has fired it stays conducting until the
  current falls low enough for the hot channel to cool. Getting it to stop at
  the right moment --- after the energy has crossed to the secondary but before
  it sloshes back, Lesson 9 --- is the hardest part of tuning a coil. It is why
  serious builders use multiple gaps in series or blow air through them.}
\end{telosfacts}

\begin{teloshazard}{Everything changes at this lesson}
The piezo demonstration is safe. The neon-sign transformer is not, and it is the
adult's alone: \qtyrange{9}{15}{kV} at \qtyrange{30}{60}{mA}, against roughly
\qty{10}{mA} across the chest to stop a heart. The boys may watch a supervised
firing from a stated distance and may do every part of the mechanical build with
the machine unplugged and the capacitor shorted. They do not touch the plug, the
switch, or anything inside the enclosure. Ozone, ultraviolet, impulsive noise and
broadband radio interference all start here too. Everything on the \textbf{Safety}
sheet applies from this moment.
\end{teloshazard}

\begin{telospanel}{The link to the coil}
The gap sets the firing voltage, and $E = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$ from Lesson 7 turns
that voltage into the energy of each shot. Widen the gap and each shot carries
more energy but fires less often; narrow it and you get frequent weak shots. That
one adjustment, plus the primary tap from Lesson 8, is most of what tuning a
Tesla coil consists of.
\end{telospanel}
